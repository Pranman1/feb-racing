\section{Executive summary}

The autonomous team has built a complete software racing platform in the last month: a
native simulator of the RoboRacer 1:10 race car, a containerised ROS~2 devkit, a starter
driving stack, a competition harness, a leaderboard and two manuals. It onboards a new member
in an hour and runs a full season (qualification, time-attack, head-to-head final) with no
hardware. Its one limit is that the code never meets a real car.

This report lays out five options for closing that gap, from staying software-only to a
1:5-scale vehicle, with a full bill of materials for each, and recommends \textbf{Plan~B: one
RoboRacer-standard 1:10 car with a Hokuyo lidar and a lab track, about \$3{,}200}. One car is
the whole ask: head-to-head racing stays in the simulator, where it already works, because the
skills built here are meant to transfer to the FSAE car, and a second small car does not
serve that. The two options the committee asked about are analysed in sections~\ref{sec:tamiya}
and~\ref{sec:large}. Converting the team's Tamiya chassis is not feasible for reasons of
eligibility and community rather than cost. A 1:5-scale car becomes affordable once the team's
own RoboSense M1 lidar and the Alameda test track are counted, but it can only be run off
campus, which rules it out as the car members learn on day to day; it is the right second
step once the 1:10 programme is running. The depth camera and the M1 lidar the team already
owns are priced at zero wherever they are used.

\subsection{Onboarding and the season}

A new member clones one repository, runs one setup command and drives manually. Copying the
starter kit and running one more command puts an autonomous car on track; the first
leaderboard entry follows the same afternoon. To qualify for the first competition a team
completes ten laps with at most one collision on every practice track. Qualified teams race a
time-attack on a secret track, and the top seeds meet in a two-car knockout final.

\section{Why hardware, and why in the off-season}
\label{sec:why}

The FSAE car is not available for autonomous work for most of the year: it is being designed,
built or raced by the rest of the team, and it is never available to an untested stack. A
1:10 car is where the autonomous team learns and validates in the meantime, on a table in the
lab, with skills that carry straight to the full-size car.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{People need to see a physical effect.} A lap time on a leaderboard keeps a
  member for a semester; a car that turns because of code they wrote keeps them for years. The
  motivation to debug a controller at midnight comes from watching it fail on a real car.
  \item \textbf{Real dynamics cannot be simulated away.} Tyre grip that varies with the floor,
  a steering servo with backlash, a battery that sags as it drains, a lidar that sees chair
  legs and reflections: the simulator models none of these faithfully, and the FSAE car has
  all of them. Members who have tuned a controller against them at 1:10 arrive at the FSAE car
  knowing what to expect.
  \item \textbf{Sim-to-real training.} The simulator's car is the digital twin of the
  RoboRacer reference vehicle. The team's method becomes: develop and race in the simulator,
  transfer to the 1:10 car, measure the gap, close it, then carry the same pipeline to the
  FSAE car. Without the car the first transfer never happens and the method is untested.
  \item \textbf{Perception on real sensors.} Cone detection and colour classification with
  the camera, lidar clustering, camera plus lidar fusion, localisation against a map: all of it
  can be built in the simulator, but only real sensors show the lighting, noise, occlusion and
  latency that decide whether it works. That is the driverless FSAE problem at small scale.
  \item \textbf{Educating new members in real hardware.} Wiring, power, safety procedures,
  motor controllers, sensor mounting and calibration, ROS~2 on an embedded computer: the
  1:10 car teaches every one of these at low cost and low risk before anyone touches the FSAE
  vehicle.
  \item \textbf{Competitions and recruiting.} RoboRacer runs physical races at ICRA, IROS
  and IFAC every year on the rules our league already uses, and a car on a table is what
  fills a room at the first meeting of the semester.
\end{itemize}

\section{The options}

Every plan includes compute, lidar, power, mounting and the track kit of section~\ref{sec:track}.
Where a part is available in more than one grade, the table names the one priced.

\subsection{Plan 0: simulator only}

Cost \$0. Everything in section~2 continues. No sim-to-real work, no physical competition,
no perception on real sensors. Kept as the base layer of every other plan, not as an answer on
its own.

\textbf{Benefits:} the exact car the league, the build guide and our simulator model; camera
plus lidar fusion is possible on day one; nothing to upgrade later. \textbf{Limits:} the lidar
is half the cost and the most fragile part; a bumper and a speed limit for new stacks are
part of the plan.

\textbf{Why not the team's RoboSense M1 on this car.} The M1 is a 3D solid-state lidar built
for full-size vehicles: about 0.7~kg against the Hokuyo's 130~g, a 120\textdegree{} forward
field of view against 270\textdegree, and a near-field blind zone of roughly half a metre in a
corridor with under a metre of free space either side. It would dominate the car's mass,
see nothing beside it, and is outside the league's sensor set. It is the right sensor for the
1:5 option below and for the FSAE car; on the 1:10 car the 2D lidar is the right tool.

\medskip
\textbf{Why it is not feasible as the team's car:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Not competition-eligible.} RoboRacer rules require a Traxxas 1:10 chassis (codes TRA74054, TRA6804R, TRA68086). A Tamiya cannot enter any physical event.
  \item \textbf{Not the simulated car.} Wheelbase, track width, tyre model and steering geometry differ from the digital twin, so simulator tuning does not transfer, which removes the main reason to have a car.
  \item \textbf{No community.} Every RoboRacer document, drawing, VESC profile and forum answer assumes the Slash. On the Tamiya the team solves mounting, power and drivetrain questions alone. That is a semester of mechanical work that teaches no autonomy.
  \item \textbf{Weak platform.} A touring car has 5~mm of ground clearance and no room for a lidar, a computer and a battery. The Slash was chosen by the community because it carries them.
\end{itemize}
The chassis is still useful as a bench mule for wiring and sensor mounting practice, at no cost.

\subsection{Plan D: a 1:5-scale vehicle with the M1 lidar, tested at Alameda}
\label{sec:large}

The committee asked about a 1:4 car. No manufacturer makes a 1:4 road car; the nearest
production platforms are 1:5 short-course trucks (Losi 5IVE-T, Arrma Kraton 8S) and 1:6
monster trucks (Traxxas X-Maxx). Two things the team already has change this option's cost:
the RoboSense M1 lidar, which is the right sensor at this scale, and access to a test track in
Alameda, which removes the need to build a track.

\toprule
Item & USD & Note \\
\midrule

8S battery pair and charger & 500 & \\

Compute: NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX class & 700 & the M1's 75{,}000 points per frame need more than an Orin Nano \\

Depth camera: Intel RealSense & 0 & owned \\

Track: Alameda test track & 0 & owned access; transport and session costs not included \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{3{,}300 to 3{,}900} & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\medskip
\textbf{What it would give the team.} A vehicle with a 3D lidar and a camera at a size where
the FSAE perception stack runs unchanged: the same sensor, the same point-cloud pipeline, the
same cone detection, on a car that can be built and crashed without touching the FSAE
vehicle. For the driverless FSAE goal this is closer to the real thing than any 1:10 car.

\textbf{Why it is not the first car, stated plainly:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It cannot be run on campus.} A 10~kg vehicle at 50~km/h needs a closed
  outdoor area, a kill switch and a marshal. Every test is a trip to Alameda with the car, a
  generator or charged packs, and a tow of tools: a session, not an afternoon in the lab. Iteration
  drops from ten runs a day to one or two a week, and the weather has a vote. New members
  cannot be handed this car to learn on.
  \item \textbf{Safety.} About 1~kJ of kinetic energy, thirty times the 1:10 car. Untested
  stacks and first-semester members do not belong near it.
  \item \textbf{No community and no competition.} No autonomous racing series exists at this
  scale. Mounts, power and motor control are solved alone.
  \item \textbf{Not the simulated car.} Nothing built in the simulator transfers until a 1:5
  vehicle model and an M1 sensor model exist in it; both are on the simulator's future list,
  and neither is small.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Verdict:} viable and worth planning for as the perception mule that sits between the
1:10 programme and the FSAE car, once there is a 1:10 car for members to learn on and a stack
worth putting on it. Not the first purchase.

\section{Sensor fusion and the driverless connection}

The team already owns an Intel RealSense depth camera. With a 2D lidar it gives the sensor pair
used by Formula Student driverless cars at small scale: the camera classifies cones by colour
and the lidar gives precise range and bearing; fusing them yields a cone map, then a track
boundary, then a raceline. The FEBAUTO simulator publishes both a front camera image and a
lidar scan on the same topics the real car will use, and its cone tracks exist for exactly
this pipeline. Plan~B supports this on day one; Plan~A's compute is enough for lidar-only
driving and can be upgraded to a Jetson for camera work later.

\section{Racing: one on one, in the simulator}

The head-to-head final is the season's finish and it stays in the simulator: two cars on one
track, each team's stack in its own container, contact costing both cars a penalty and a
respawn, finishing order by laps then time. The simulator already does this, so no second
physical car is asked for. The figures show a race on Spielberg and on the FEB Loop between
the starter kit and the house racer.

\section{Recommendation and timeline}

Fund Plan~B (\$3{,}190 plus 10\,\% contingency, \$3{,}500). Order the lidar first; its
lead time is the longest. Revisit Plan~D, the 1:5 car with the M1, once the 1:10 programme has
run a season and the simulator carries a 1:5 vehicle and M1 sensor model.

\section{Risks}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Crashes.} Bumper, soft duct walls, a speed limit for new stacks; spares budgeted. The lidar is the fragile part and sits behind the bumper line.
  \item \textbf{Batteries.} LiPo bag, supervised charging, a written procedure before the first charge.
  \item \textbf{Supply.} Hokuyo lead times vary; every other part is stocked by hobby and electronics retailers.
  \item \textbf{Access.} The car lives with the autonomous lead; access follows the simulator's qualification rule, so an untested stack never has the car as its first target.
\end{itemize}
